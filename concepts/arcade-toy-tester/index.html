    Arcade: Toy tester

    Toy tester (game type toy-tester) is a shop you run. Brands write to you offering a product from your own catalogue to test; you film the review on your webcam, and the product then sits on one of your shelves for a few days, selling to customers and paying you a commission. That commission buys more shelves, a bigger cut and campaigns.

    Listings expire, so a shop you stop filming for slowly empties out. That is the pressure the whole game runs on.

    Unlike the other arcade games, this one is a save, not a run: the shop lives on the server and is still there next time. You can keep several shops side by side.

    The loop

    1. Open the shop — nothing has moved since you left; the day only ends when you say so
    2. Read the offers — a brand, a product, a brief and a fee
    3. Take it or leave it — turning an offer down costs standing; paying the kill fee does not
    4. Film the test — the webcam records while you work through the brief
    5. Price it — the listing goes up at whatever you ask
    6. Sell — the next time you open the shop, that listing pays out
    7. Spend — shelves, your cut, or the size of the shop itself

    Shops and the test shop

    You can run more than one shop. Each keeps its own money, shelves, inbox, standing and history. Click the shop name at the top of the shop to get back to the picker, where you switch between shops, open another or delete one. Switching closes the books on the shop you switch to, exactly as opening one does.

    Deleting a shop takes its shelves, its archive and every review you filmed for it — the video files are removed from storage, not just the entries. You can delete your last shop; you simply land back on the picker with nothing open.

    A brand new shop starts with 120 in the bank, three empty shelves and standing 50, which is worth four offers waiting in the inbox straight away. No receipt prints on that first visit — there is nothing to settle yet.

    A shop named admin is a test shop

    Name the tester admin when creating a shop and it opens with every upgrade already bought, maximum standing and a balance you cannot spend through. It exists so the whole game can be reached without playing up to it, and it works on any install — unlike a dev-only endpoint, which is what OnlyCams uses.

    Offers

    What gets tested is always a toy. Offers are drawn from the Toys group of your catalogue — the dildos, the plugs, the chastity cages, the vibrators. The rest of your catalogue is not ignored; it turns up as extras on the brief. Products already on a shelf or already in the inbox are never offered again while they are there, so the same item is never in front of you twice.

    An offer carries one job and one to three extras:

    • The job follows from what kind of toy it is. A plug gets asked to go in and out five times, or to stay in for the whole review. A large dildo gets asked to go all the way down to the base, or to be ridden in standing doggy. A chastity cage gets locked on, on camera. A vibrator gets used until you cannot keep still. The job is what you were sent the toy for, so it can never be bought out of the brief.
    • The extras are chosen to suit that job rather than at random. A mouth test comes with lipstick and lashes; hands come with nails, rings and a bracelet; riding comes with heels and stockings; a cage comes with a vibrator used on it, and a sissygasm. That pairing is deliberate — an offer should read like something a person thought up.

    Extras that name a category resolve to a real item from your collection with its photo and name, matched on the taxonomy category first and on the product name second, so a half-sorted catalogue still works. Where nothing fits, the generic wording stands.

    Wardrobe and product extras can be bought out of a brief. The ones that are really the job — grinding on it, showing your tape, finishing on camera — cannot.

    A catalogue without toys still plays

    If your catalogue has no toys to offer — it is empty, you only have wardrobe in it, or every toy you own is already on a shelf or in the inbox — the game falls back to built-in sample stock so the shop is never left with nothing to do. Samples cover one of each toy kind plus a small wardrobe for the briefs to name. They are marked everywhere they appear, they carry no photos, and the shop shows a line pointing at your catalogue. Fill it and the brands go back to offering your own things.

    Extras always prefer your real collection, even when the toy being tested is a sample — those are your own clothes.

    The inbox is topped up when you open the shop, when you clear an offer (turning it down or paying it off) and when you deliver a test, so you can never empty it and get stuck.

    Each offer carries one to three briefs — conditions the brand wants met on camera. Briefs that name a product category resolve to a real item from your collection, with its photo and name; if that category is empty, the generic wording stands.

    What you can do Cost Effect
    Film it — Opens the shoot; needs a free shelf
    Turn down 6 standing The offer is gone
    Pay it off 40 The offer is gone and your standing holds
    Drop a brief 60 Removes one wardrobe or product condition, cuts the required footage by a third and the fee by a fifth

    Your standing (0–100, starts at 50) decides how many offers wait for you and how well they pay. Under 30 the inbox thins to two; over 70 it runs to six and the fees are roughly triple what they were at the bottom. Delivering a test adds 4.

    Offers expire three days after they arrive.

    Filming

    The shoot is a full-screen camera with the brief pinned in the corner as a shot list you tick off, and a counter showing how much footage you have against what was asked.

    Nothing records until you press Record. From there you can hold the take with Pause and pick it back up; the counter only runs while the camera does, so it always reads as footage shot rather than time spent on the screen. The recording uploads to the server in five-second chunks while you film, so nothing is held in the browser; when you wrap, the server repairs the container and cuts a thumbnail.

    There are three ways to make the review, picked on the shoot screen itself:

    Source How it works
    This camera The webcam on the machine you are playing on. The camera picker at the bottom lists everything available
    My phone Scan the QR with your phone; its camera arrives over WebRTC as just another entry in that picker, and is selected for you the moment it connects. This works even when you refused the laptop camera — the phone can be the only camera you have
    A file A video you already filmed. It takes the place of the recording — nothing is captured here, and its own length counts as the review length

    Whichever you use, the rest is identical: the file is finalized, a thumbnail is cut, and the product goes on the shelf with the review attached.

    You can stop early. Short reviews simply sell worse — and if you deliver under 60% of the requested footage, a quarter of that listing's commission is clawed back as returns.

    How things sell

    Every day you close, each listing draws visitors and converts a fraction of them.

    visitors   = shop traffic × freshness × campaign
conversion = 10% × price factor × review factor    (capped at 35%)
sales      = visitors × conversion
you get    = sales × asking price × your cut
    Term What drives it
    Shop traffic 40 visitors, plus 25 per shop level above the first
    Freshness Full on its first day, three quarters on the second, half after that
    Price factor Asking under the catalogue price sells faster, over it slower (0.3–2.0)
    Review factor 0.4 at nothing filmed, 1.0 at the full requested length, plus 0.2 if every brief was met
    Campaign A bought campaign multiplies traffic by a roll between 0.8 and 2.2 — it can flop

    There is deliberately no floor under the conversion rate: a listing nobody would buy sells nothing. Asking prices are held between a quarter and triple the catalogue price, because outside that band the price lever would beat every other decision in the game.

    Products without a price in your catalogue fall back to the median of their category, then to a flat 35.

    The settlement

    Opening the shop closes the books and prints a receipt: every listing's sales and commission, any returns, the shelf rent, and what it came to. Rent is 8 per occupied shelf per day, so a shelf holding something that does not sell costs you money.

    Listings run for 3 days and then come off the shelf into the archive, where their page and review stay readable until you delete them.

    Spending

    Upgrade Steps Prices
    Shelves 3 → 7 150 · 400 · 900 · 2000
    Your cut 8% → 11% → 14% → 17% → 20% 250 · 600 · 1200 · 2500
    Shop size Level 1 → 5 (more visitors per listing) 500 · 1500 · 4000 · 9000
    Campaign Per listing, per day A tenth of the catalogue price, minimum 25

    Your reviews

    The Reviews section holds every review this shop has filmed — the ones on the shelf and the ones whose listing has run out. Each one plays back in place, and carries what it sold and whether the brief was met.

    From here a review can be exported to your video collection: give it a name, add tags and pick actors, and it goes through the normal video import as a job. The shop keeps its own copy, so the product page still plays it. A review whose listing has run out can also be deleted here, file and all.

    The product page

    Every listing gets a real storefront page: the product's own photo and price from your catalogue, your recorded review, and the customer reviews underneath. It is the one bright screen in the game — the rest of the shop is the back office — and it stays readable after the listing expires.

    Customers write according to how the review was filmed and how it was priced, and now and then one asks for something else to be tested next.

    Where it is stored

    The shop is JSON on the player's UserGameSettings row, exactly like OnlyCams keeps its profile; a shoot is a GameSession. Both tables already existed, so this game needed no schema change. Review recordings live in normal file storage and can be deleted per listing from the archive.
